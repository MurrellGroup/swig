# 12. Antibody histories and vaccine design

A [vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaccine) presents material that can induce an immune response and establish protection or useful memory. The material that elicits the response is an **immunogen**. Its molecular shape, delivery, persistence, and accompanying inflammatory signals influence which cells respond and how the response develops.

For antibody responses, the sequence of events described earlier remains central: receptors bind antigen, B cells obtain activation and helper signals, responding cells expand, and some descendants enter memory or antibody-secreting compartments. An [adjuvant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunologic_adjuvant) can change the immune environment in which this happens. ([R02](references.md#r02), [R05](references.md#r05), [R07](references.md#r07))

## Defining the desired antibody activity

Binding an antigen is one measurable property. A [neutralizing antibody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutralizing_antibody) reduces infectivity in an appropriate assay, for example by blocking a viral interaction needed for cell entry. Other antibodies can contribute through Fc-dependent functions.

**Potency** describes the concentration needed to achieve a specified effect under the assay conditions. **Breadth** describes how widely the effect extends across a defined collection of variants or strains. These quantities require a stated assay and test panel. Binding affinity to one protein supplies useful information but leaves those functional measurements to be made separately.

An antibody that neutralizes many diverse HIV strains is often called a **broadly neutralizing antibody**, or bnAb. Some such antibodies isolated after infection have extensive mutation histories. Reconstructing those histories can identify candidate starting receptors and intermediate sequences for experiments. ([R20](references.md#r20))

## A mature antibody and its precursor can prefer different antigens

An antibody isolated late in a response contains the changes that survived its lineage's history. The naïve receptor that started the lineage can bind the same antigen with a different affinity, recognize a different presentation of it, or bind too weakly to be readily recruited under the conditions of an immunization.

This creates a design problem. An immunogen chosen because it binds a desired mature antibody may interact poorly with the naïve B cells from which a similar response would have to start. Testing reconstructed ancestors and early intermediates helps identify that gap.

In a longitudinal study of the HIV antibody lineage CH103, Liao and colleagues combined antibody sequences and functional measurements with sequences of the evolving virus. Their reconstruction connected changes in the antibody family with changes in its antigenic environment. This provides an example of using an antibody history to identify biologically meaningful stages for further testing. ([R20](references.md#r20))

## Germline-targeting immunogens

**Germline targeting** is an immunogen-design strategy aimed at engaging naïve B cells with receptor features associated with a desired type of mature antibody. The immunogen is engineered to bind those starting receptors sufficiently well to help recruit them into a response. The term refers to receptor features at the beginning of maturation; their junctions have already been generated by recombination.

A particular donor's inferred UCA is one candidate starting sequence. Across people, suitable naïve receptors can differ in inherited alleles, junctions, and partner chains. A broadly useful priming immunogen must therefore be evaluated against an appropriate range of real precursor receptors, including their frequency and competition with other B cells.

Leggat and colleagues reported a human phase 1 trial in 2022 in which the engineered eOD-GT8 60mer immunogen recruited B-cell precursors with features of the VRC01 class of HIV antibodies and induced early maturation. VRC01-class antibodies share receptor features and a common mode of HIV recognition. The study tested recruitment and early response properties; protective efficacy and mature breadth were separate questions. ([R19](references.md#r19))

## Sequential immunization

A priming immunogen can recruit a suitable starting population. Later **boosts** can present related but altered antigens intended to favour useful descendant variants. This is called sequential immunization when the immunogens are deliberately chosen as a series.

The underlying mechanism is selection on variation. SHM generates receptor variants; the next antigenic environment changes which of those variants can capture antigen and obtain help. A design can favour particular functional directions while leaving the exact mutations and branching outcomes to the stochastic biology of each response. ([R10](references.md#r10), [R20](references.md#r20))

Reconstructed intermediates help ask whether a proposed boost can engage receptors likely to exist after the previous immunization. A boost that binds only a much later endpoint may leave a gap in that sequence of accessible responses. Conversely, strong binding to an intermediate may support recruitment without selecting the desired breadth. Both binding and functional assays contribute to choosing a sequence of immunogens.

Practical outcomes also depend on precursor abundance, competing clones, antigen display, T-cell help, and the persistence of responding cells. Repeating an antigen can expand an existing response, while changing it can favour a different subset. The results need to be measured in the resulting repertoire and antibody activities.

## How sequence analyses contribute to an experimental study

The study can begin with a functionally characterized antibody: its paired chain sequences, a defined binding target, and measurements across a relevant pathogen panel. Related sequences collected from the same donor then provide candidates for a lineage history.

V(D)J assignment and donor-reference review establish plausible inherited segments. Careful grouping and sequence curation define the family. Alignments and trees organize its variants; UCA and intermediate reconstruction propose earlier receptors. Expression and functional testing of these molecules reveal which activities are compatible with each reconstructed stage.

Those observations can guide immunogen design and selection. A candidate priming immunogen is tested against diverse naïve precursors. Candidate boosts are tested against plausible intermediate receptors and then in immunization studies. Repertoire sequencing measures which families actually responded, whether they persisted, and which mutations and chain combinations appeared.

At each stage, the computational result narrows or organizes an experimental question. A tree can suggest which variants to express. A posterior can identify which ancestral alternatives deserve testing. A repertoire comparison can reveal an unintended dominant response. Protection ultimately requires evidence from the appropriate biological and clinical studies.

## Where T cells enter the design

T-cell help is integral to many affinity-matured antibody responses. A B cell recognizes an intact feature of the immunogen, internalizes material, and presents derived peptides to helper T cells. The helper response therefore depends on peptide generation, MHC class II presentation, and the responding T-cell repertoire. These requirements concern a different recognition surface from the antibody's epitope. ([R02](references.md#r02), [R06](references.md#r06))

Vaccines can also aim to induce protective T-cell responses directly. In that setting, receptor sequencing can track responding TCR clonotypes across time and tissues. Determining their peptide–MHC targets and functional effects requires additional specificity and cellular assays. The usual stability of TCR sequence makes clonal abundance and persistence more central than an antibody-like SHM trajectory.

## What to retain with a reconstructed-antibody result

An experimentally used ancestor should remain traceable to its observed family, paired-chain evidence, alignment, references, tree, model settings, and alternative supported sequences. Report the functional results for the molecules actually expressed.

When several plausible ancestors give the same experimental conclusion, that agreement makes the conclusion less dependent on a particular reconstruction. When they differ, the uncertainty identifies a biological question that additional sequence or experimental evidence can help resolve.
