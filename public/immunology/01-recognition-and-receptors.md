# 1. Recognition and receptors

An immune response involves several kinds of cells working together. Some detect broad signs of infection or tissue damage; others recognize particular molecular structures. The [innate immune system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innate_immune_system) supplies early detection, inflammation, and many of the signals that organize a response. The [adaptive immune system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptive_immune_system) includes B and T cells, whose receptors vary extensively from cell to cell. Both systems contribute to protection. ([R01](references.md#r01), [R07](references.md#r07))

## Antigens and epitopes

An [antigen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigen) is a molecular target that an antibody or antigen receptor can recognize. Antigens include proteins from viruses and bacteria, carbohydrates on microbial surfaces, and molecules produced by our own cells. Whether recognition leads to an immune response depends on the cells and signals involved.

A receptor usually contacts a particular part of an antigen, called an [epitope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epitope). A large protein can contain many epitopes. For an antibody, an epitope may be a patch on the surface of a folded protein. Amino acids far apart along the protein's sequence can lie next to one another in that patch.

Binding depends on the fit and chemistry of the two surfaces: shape, charge, hydrogen bonding, and other molecular interactions. A receptor may bind several related structures, sometimes with quite different strengths. This is [cross-reactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-reactivity). Antigen recognition is therefore a matter of measurable interactions, with a range of strengths and consequences. ([R01](references.md#r01))

## B cells and antibodies

A [B cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B_cell) carries many copies of a [B-cell receptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-cell_receptor), or BCR. Its antigen-binding component is a membrane-bound [immunoglobulin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibody), associated with proteins that transmit a signal into the cell. The receptor can bind an accessible antigen directly.

When activated, some B cells develop into antibody-secreting cells, including [plasma cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_cell). Their secreted immunoglobulins are [antibodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibody). Antibodies can bind targets away from the cell that produced them: in blood, tissues, or secretions such as mucus. A BCR and an antibody made from the same variable-region sequences have the same antigen-binding components, while their membrane attachment and mode of action differ. ([R07](references.md#r07))

A conventional antibody molecule contains two identical [heavy chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunoglobulin_heavy_chain) and two identical [light chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunoglobulin_light_chain). Each of its two binding sites is formed jointly by one heavy-chain variable domain and one light-chain variable domain. A light chain is either kappa, written κ, or lambda, written λ.

The familiar Y-shaped antibody diagram represents this arrangement. The ends of the arms bind antigen. Much of the stem belongs to the [Fc region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fragment_crystallizable_region), which can interact with immune cells or soluble immune proteins. The heavy-chain constant region determines the antibody's class, such as IgM or IgG. Chapter 3 explains how a B-cell family can produce different classes. ([R03](references.md#r03), [R08](references.md#r08))

## T cells and presented antigen

A [T cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T_cell) also carries many copies of an antigen receptor. Most T cells have a [T-cell receptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-cell_receptor), or TCR, made from an alpha chain and a beta chain. A smaller population uses gamma and delta chains.

Most alpha–beta TCRs recognize a short protein fragment, called a [peptide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peptide), held by a [major histocompatibility complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_histocompatibility_complex) molecule on another cell. This is [antigen presentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigen_presentation). The TCR contacts the peptide–MHC complex as a whole. In humans, MHC molecules are also called [human leukocyte antigens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_leukocyte_antigen), or HLA molecules.

This arrangement lets a T cell inspect material processed by another cell. An infected cell can display peptides derived from viral proteins; a dendritic cell can display material it has collected from its surroundings. T-cell activation then changes the behaviour of the T cell, which may help other immune cells or kill an infected target. Chapter 4 develops these pathways and introduces some exceptions to conventional peptide–MHC recognition. ([R06](references.md#r06))

## Variable regions, frameworks, and CDRs

Each antibody or TCR chain has a **variable region** and a **constant region**. Variable regions differ greatly between receptors and form the antigen-recognition surface. Constant regions provide other structural and functional features.

Within each variable domain, three loops are especially variable. They are the [complementarity-determining regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementarity-determining_region), abbreviated **CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3**. The intervening [framework regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framework_region) support the domain's folded structure. Each binding site brings together CDRs from both receptor chains.

CDR3 is particularly diverse because it crosses the DNA junction where gene segments were joined during receptor assembly. For this reason, CDR3 sequences are useful when comparing receptors. Binding also depends on the other CDRs, framework residues, the partner chain, and the resulting three-dimensional structure. ([R03](references.md#r03), [R04](references.md#r04))

## A repertoire is a population of receptors

An [immune repertoire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immune_repertoire) is the collection of receptors represented in a population of lymphocytes. A blood sample contains only part of a person's repertoire. Different tissues and cell types can contain different mixtures of receptors.

A receptor-sequencing experiment often reads one chain at a time. A heavy-chain sequence provides information about one component of an antibody. Recovering its naturally paired light chain is an additional experimental step. The same issue arises for TCR alpha and beta chains.

The first useful question about a sequence is therefore: **which receptor chain does it represent, and how was it sampled?** The next chapter explains how that chain's variable-region DNA was assembled.
